Hakeem Jeffries’ record

Not rumors. Not talking points. FEC filings, House roll calls and his own words, each linked to its source, each with his explanation beside it. This is the record House Democrats are about to reward with the gavel.

  1. $421,396 through AIPAC’s PAC

    From February 2025 to September 2026, donors routed $421,396 through AIPAC’s political action committee to Jeffries’ campaign and two joint fundraising committees he heads. One donor sent $149,300 on May 20, 2026, alone.

    The money is individual donors’ contributions, earmarked through the PAC, not the PAC’s own funds. The two joint committees also raise money for the DCCC and other House Democrats.

    Source: FEC, AIPAC PAC disbursements, 2025-2026

  2. His caucus split. He voted no.

    On July 15, 2026, the House rejected Rep. Thomas Massie’s amendment to cut Israel’s $3.3 billion in Foreign Military Financing, 104-314. More Democrats voted for it (103) than against it (98). Jeffries voted no.

    His explanation, the day before: the amendment was “overly broad” and would limit humanitarian aid, refugee resettlement and embassy funds. He also wrote that “American policy in the Middle East must change.”

    Sources: House roll call 243; Jeffries’ Dear Colleague letter, July 14, 2026; FMEP

  3. “Don’t want us to get even”

    The morning after the election, Jeffries said on MS NOW’s “Morning Joe”: “The American people don’t want us to get even; they want us to get things done on their behalf because life is way too expensive.”

    In August he named the Judiciary and Oversight committees to lead on accountability and declined to rule impeachment in or out.

    Source: FOX 5 DC, Nov. 4, 2026

  4. Four months to back his city’s nominee

    Zohran Mamdani won New York City’s Democratic mayoral primary in June 2025. Jeffries, who represents Brooklyn, held off for months and endorsed him on Oct. 24, 2025.

    His statement cited “areas of principled disagreement” and said he respected the will of primary voters.

    Source: The Associated Press, Oct. 24, 2025

  5. “Exactly the right outcome”

    When President Trump gave Rep. Henry Cuellar, D-Texas, a preemptive pardon on federal bribery charges in December 2025, Jeffries told CNN: “I think the outcome was exactly the right outcome.”

    His reason: “this indictment was very thin to begin with, in my view.”

    Source: The Hill, Dec. 3, 2025

  6. A PAC to shield incumbents from primaries

    In June 2021, Jeffries, Josh Gottheimer and Terri Sewell launched Team Blue PAC to defend House Democratic incumbents facing primary challenges.

    Jeffries called it “an extension of my job as caucus chair: to ensure that members are successful.”

    Source: NBC News, June 26, 2021

  7. Medicare for All, until he led

    Jeffries cosponsored the Medicare for All Act in 2019 and was an original cosponsor in 2021. He did not cosponsor the 2023 version, introduced four months after he became Democratic leader.

    In August 2026 he told NBC’s “Meet the Press”: “I haven’t cosponsored the Medicare for All legislation that has been introduced over the last several years.” He said Democrats should focus on “driving down the high cost of living.”

    Sources: congress.gov cosponsor lists for H.R. 1384 (2019), H.R. 1976 (2021) and H.R. 3421 (2023); Newsweek

  8. His caucus split again. He voted yes.

    On July 17, 2025, the House passed the GENIUS Act, which sets federal rules for stablecoins, 308-122. House Democrats voted 110 to 102 against it. Jeffries voted yes, and the bill went to President Trump, whose family launched a company that issues a stablecoin, Politico reported.

    No public statement from Jeffries explaining this vote has turned up. If he gave one, send it and it goes here.

    Sources: House roll call 200; Common Dreams, quoting Politico, July 17, 2025

  9. $188,600 from defense-tech executives

    The chief executive of defense contractor Anduril Industries gave $100,000 to a joint fundraising committee Jeffries heads in April 2026, and Anduril’s co-founder and chief operating officer gave $44,300 to another in August. A manager at data-analytics company Palantir gave $44,300 in February 2025.

    No public statement from Jeffries about these gifts has turned up. For scale: his campaign raised $12.7 million from individuals in 2025-26 through Oct. 14, and $8.1 million of it came from donors who gave $200 or less. The joint committees also raise money for other House Democrats.

    Sources: FEC receipts from Anduril and Palantir employees; Jeffries for Congress, 2025-26 totals; first reported by Sludge, Nov. 4, 2026

  10. “Bend the knee”

    In August 2021, Jeffries told The Atlantic: “There will never be a moment where I bend the knee to hard-left democratic socialism.”

    In the same interview: “There’s a difference between progressive Democrats and hard-left democratic socialists. It’s not a distinction that I’m drawing. They draw that distinction.” He called himself “a Black progressive Democrat concerned with addressing racial and social and economic injustice with the fierce urgency of now.”

    Source: The Atlantic, Aug. 10, 2021

Demand a real vote

The speaker sets the House agenda and stands second in line to the presidency, behind only the vice president. A job that powerful should be won in an open contest, not handed down. In 20 speaker ballots since 2023, no House Democrat has voted for anyone but Jeffries. A real contest makes every candidate answer for the agenda before anyone gets the gavel.

The speaker vote, explained

When does the House elect the next speaker?

When the 120th Congress first meets. The 20th Amendment sets that for noon on Jan. 3, 2027, unless Congress picks another day by law, and the House traditionally elects its speaker by roll call as soon as it convenes. House Democrats choose their nominee first, behind closed doors; ABC News and Bloomberg Government report that vote is set for Nov. 18.

Sources: 20th Amendment, National Archives; Congressional Research Service, RL30857; reports on the caucus vote

How many votes does it take to become speaker?

A majority of the votes cast for a person by name. That is 218 only if all 435 members vote for someone; absences and “present” votes lower the number needed.

Source: Congressional Research Service, RL30857

Can a Democrat vote for someone other than Jeffries?

Yes. Each party’s caucus picks a nominee, but on the floor a member may vote for anyone, nominated or not. In 14 of the 17 speaker elections since 1997, some members voted for someone other than their party’s nominee. In all 20 speaker ballots since 2023, no House Democrat has.

Sources: Congressional Research Service, RL30857; House roll calls

Does the speaker have to be a member of the House?

No. The Constitution does not require it, but every speaker so far has been a member.

Source: Congressional Research Service, RL30857

Why does the speaker matter?

The speaker is second in line to the presidency, behind only the vice president, under federal law, and leads the House.

Source: 3 U.S.C. § 19
